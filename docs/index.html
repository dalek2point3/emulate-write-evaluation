It's a story that is well-known in its broad outlines. When Mohandas Gandhi arrived in Durban in May 1893, he was a young lawyer who had been hired to help Dada Abdulla, an Indian merchant. He thought he would be staying only for a year, at most. It was only a few days later, when he was thrown out of the first-class compartment of a train to Pietermaritzburg that he became aware of the nature of racial discrimination in South Africa. But even then, he did not imagine that he would spend the rest of his life fighting against racial inequality in South Africa. It was only when he was about to return to India because the work he had been hired to do was nearly completed that he learned that the Legislative Council of Natal was about to pass a law curbing the right of Indians to vote. He helped mobilise opposition to this and thus decided to stay on. In 1894 he founded the Natal Indian Congress and began the long political struggle to remove discriminatory laws, largely on the grounds that Indians, both the merchants and the indentured labourers, had rights as British subjects. This may help explain why, during the Anglo-Boer War and the Zulu rebellion in 1906, Gandhi worked to enrol Indians to serve in an ambulance corps and as stretcher-bearers in support of the British Empire. Gandhi developed the technique of satyagraha – force of truth, or soul-force – in response to the Transvaal government's attempt to pass the Asiatic Registration Act in 1906.
